# Literature review

For: *Is Huang's Law Moore's Law? Decomposing a Decade of AI Accelerator Performance Growth (2012–2026)*.

Compiled 2026-10-29. There are 31 sources, and each was opened on that date; for papers, title, authors, year,
venue and DOI were also checked against Crossref or arXiv. Every number quoted below comes from the source it is
attributed to, and from the version stated. BibTeX is in `paper/refs.bib` and uses the keys given in brackets.
29 of the sources are cited in the paper; the others (marked *not cited*) were read for context or as data
sources. Where only a third-party copy of a paywalled text could be read, the entry says so.

Each entry ends with how it relates to this paper:
- **Precedent**: prior work that measures the same or nearly the same thing.
- **Contrast**: a different measurement of the same question.
- **Method**: a source for a design or measurement choice.
- **Context**: background for the introduction or discussion.

## 1. Moore's law, Dennard scaling and the efficiency of computing

**Moore, G. E. (1965), *Electronics* 38(8):114–117 — "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits"** [`moore1965`]
Moore's empirical claim is about the *cost-optimal* number of components per chip, not about performance: "The complexity for minimum component costs has increased at a rate of roughly a factor of two per year (see graph)." He adds: "Certainly over the short term this rate can be expected to continue, if not to increase." He also projects that the rate will "remain nearly constant for at least ten years", and that "by 1975, the number of components per integrated circuit for minimum cost will be 65 000." He puts the cost minimum at the time "when 50 components are used per circuit", and already asks about heat: "Will it be possible to remove the heat generated by tens of thousands of components in a single silicon chip?" *Relation: precedent.* This is the source of the "Moore's law" baseline that the paper's density (and N·f) terms are measured against. Note that the original law counts components at minimum cost per chip, which is closer to the paper's N = density × area than to density alone. *Checked:* text read in the official reprint, Appendix C of NRC (2011) *The Future of Computing Performance* (book pp. 169–173; reprint note "Electronics, pp. 114-117, April 19, 1965"): https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12980/chapter/12 . The original 1965 magazine issue has no DOI. Crossref confirms the two IEEE reprints: Proc. IEEE 86(1):82–85, 1998, doi:10.1109/JPROC.1998.658762, and IEEE SSCS Newsletter 11(3):33–35, 2006, doi:10.1109/N-SSC.2006.4785860, whose title says "Reprinted from Electronics, volume 38, number 8, April 19, 1965, pp.114 ff." Access date 2026-10-29.

**Moore, G. E. (1975), *IEEE IEDM Technical Digest* pp. 11–13 — "Progress in digital integrated electronics"** [`moore1975`]
The paper opens: "Complexity of integrated circuits has approximately doubled every year since their introduction." It then decomposes the growth multiplicatively, in effect the same move as this paper. Over 1959–1975, "Chip area for maximum complexity has increased by a factor of approximately 20". Finer dimensions give "the increase in density from this source over the 1959-1975 period is a factor of approximately 32". Area and geometry together give a "640-fold increase in complexity", "leaving a factor of about 100 to account for through 1975, as is shown graphically in Figure 4". Moore attributes that residual to "circuit and device cleverness" and suggests "a limit to the contribution of circuit and device cleverness of another factor of four in component density." His forecast: "The new slope might approximate a doubling every two years, rather than every year, by the end of the decade." *Relation: method (precedent).* Moore 1975 is the earliest multiplicative decomposition of chip-level growth into area, density and a residual "cleverness" term. It is the direct ancestor of this paper's density × area × clock × specialization × precision identity and worth citing as such. *Checked:* text read as OCR of a reprint ("Reprinted, with permission, from Technical Digest 1975", "© 1975 IEEE") filed as a public PTAB exhibit (IPR2015-01201, Ex. 2024): https://www.docketalarm.com/cases/PTAB/IPR2015-01201/Inter_Partes_Review_of_U.S._Pat._5591678/03-11-2016-Patent_Owner/Exhibit-2024-Exhibit_2024/ (only the first three pages are visible there). An image-only scan was also fetched from uio.no but could not be read as text. Crossref finds no DOI for the 1975 original. It confirms the IEEE reprint: IEEE SSCS Newsletter 11(3):36–37, Sept. 2006, doi:10.1109/N-SSC.2006.4804410, whose title gives "Technical Digest. International Electron Devices Meeting, IEEE, 1975, pp. 11-13". Access date 2026-10-29.

**Dennard, R. H., Gaensslen, F. H., Yu, H.-N., Rideout, V. L., Bassous, E., & LeBlanc, A. R. (1974), *IEEE Journal of Solid-State Circuits* 9(5):256–268 — "Design of ion-implanted MOSFET's with very small physical dimensions"** [`dennard1974`]
The scaling rule says "all linear dimensions are reduced by a unit less scaling factor" κ, "the voltages applied to the device are reduced by the same factor", and "the substrate doping concentration is increased, again using the same scaling factor". The circuit-level results are in Table I ("Scaling Results for Circuit Performance"): delay per circuit falls by 1/κ and power per circuit by 1/κ². Hence "Since the area of a given device or circuit is also reduced by κ2, the power density remains constant." *Relation: context.* Constant power density is what let density gains turn into clock gains "for free". Its breakdown is why the paper's clock term is nearly flat and performance per watt grows more slowly than headline throughput. *Checked:* full text read in the official reprint, Appendix D of NRC (2011) (Table 1 is on book p. 183): https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12980/chapter/13 . DOI 10.1109/JSSC.1974.1050511 verified via Crossref (vol. 9, no. 5, pp. 256–268, Oct. 1974, six authors). The IEEE Xplore page https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1050511 returned no readable content. Access date 2026-10-29.

**Esmaeilzadeh, H., Blem, E., St. Amant, R., Sankaralingam, K., & Burger, D. (2011), *Proc. 38th Annual International Symposium on Computer Architecture (ISCA '11)*, pp. 365–376 — "Dark silicon and the end of multicore scaling"** [`esmaeilzadeh2011`]
The abstract states: "The failure of Dennard scaling, to which the shift to multicore parts is partially a response, may soon limit multicore scaling just as single-core scaling has been curtailed." The power-limited model, built from "measurements from over 150 processors", finds that "Even at 22 nm (just one year from now), 21% of a fixed-size chip must be powered off, and at 8 nm, this number grows to more than 50%." "Through 2024, only 7.9× average speedup is possible across commonly used parallel workloads, leaving a nearly 24-fold gap from a target of doubled performance per generation" (Abstract). *Relation: context.* Dark silicon is the mechanism by which transistors stop converting into throughput under a power cap. It motivates why accelerator gains must come from ops per transistor per cycle (specialization) and narrower precision rather than from N·f. *Checked:* author-hosted PDF (University of Wisconsin Vertical Research group), https://research.cs.wisc.edu/vertical/papers/2011/isca11-darksilicon.pdf . DOI 10.1145/2000064.2000108 verified via Crossref (proceedings-article, pp. 365–376, ACM, 4 June 2011). Access date 2026-10-29.

**Koomey, J. G., Berard, S., Sanchez, M., & Wong, H. (2011), *IEEE Annals of the History of Computing* 33(3):46–54 — "Implications of historical trends in the electrical efficiency of computing"** [`koomey2011`]
Peak computations per kWh "doubled every 1.57 years over the entire analysis period" (p. 48). The Fig. 3 caption (p. 49) reads "Efficiency doubled every 1.57 years from 1946 to 2009", and the Fig. 4 caption (p. 50) reads "Efficiency doubled every 1.52 years from 1975 to 2009" for PCs alone. PC performance per computer shows "a doubling time of 1.50 years from 1975" (p. 48). The paper also notes that "In 1975, Moore modified his observation to a doubling of complexity every two years" (p. 46). The metric is explicitly peak-based: the data "measure computing efficiency at peak performance" (Fig. 3 caption). *Relation: precedent / contrast.* Koomey's law is the classic peak-efficiency trend. The paper's 1.63-year doubling of accelerator performance per watt can be set directly against Koomey's 1.57 and 1.52 years, with one caveat: if the paper's figure is computed at headline (reduced) precision, it includes precision changes that Koomey's fixed-workload metric does not. *Checked:* abstract and context on the first author's blog, https://www.koomey.com/koomey_blog/technology-review-reported-today-on-our-computing-efficiency-trends-analysis (quotes the abstract: "doubling every 1.5 years"). Full text read from a third-party mirror of the publisher PDF (page headers "IEEE Annals of the History of Computing … July–September 2011", pp. 46–52): https://www.gwern.net/doc/cs/hardware/2011-koomey.pdf . DOI 10.1109/MAHC.2010.28 verified via Crossref (33(3):46–54). Access date 2026-10-29.

**Nagy, B., Farmer, J. D., Bui, Q. M., & Trancik, J. E. (2013), *PLoS ONE* 8(2):e52669 — "Statistical basis for predicting technological progress"** [`nagy2013`]
Using "a new database on the cost and production of 62 different technologies", the authors compare forecasting rules by hindcasting: "Wright's law produces the best forecasts, but Moore's law is not far behind." They find that "production tends to increase exponentially", so exponential cost decline plus exponential production "would make Moore's law and Wright's law indistinguishable, as originally pointed out by Sahal." Forecast errors grow, with "the square root of the logarithmic error growing linearly with the forecasting horizon at a typical rate of 2.5% per year" (all from the Abstract). On time trends they caution that "The agreement with exponential behavior ranges from very good to rather poor" because "these are short time series and some of them are very noisy", and that Moore's law is "a reasonable approximation only over short time spans" (Results and Discussion). *Relation: method.* This supports fitting log-linear time trends (doubling times) to technology metrics and treating them as forecastable, while warning about short and noisy series. That warning applies to a 14-year, 79-chip accelerator panel. *Checked:* https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0052669 (publisher, open access). DOI verified via Crossref (8(2):e52669, 28 Feb 2013). Access date 2026-10-29.

## 2. Specialization after scaling

**Leiserson, C. E., Thompson, N. C., Emer, J. S., Kuszmaul, B. C., Lampson, B. W., Sanchez, D., & Schardl, T. B. (2020), *Science* 368(6495):eaam9744 — "There's plenty of room at the Top: What will drive computer performance after Moore's law?"** [`leiserson2020`]
The abstract says that "As miniaturization approaches its limits, bringing an end to Moore's law, performance gains will need to come from software, algorithms, and hardware". These post-Moore gains "will be opportunistic, uneven, and sporadic, and they will suffer from the law of diminishing returns." In the Hardware architecture section (Fig. 2, p. 4 of 7), "clock speed increased by a factor of more than 200 from 1985 to 2005, when it plateaued owing to the end of Dennard scaling". The authors "do not think that average clock speed will increase after Moore's law ends". Domain-specific hardware "can be much more streamlined and use many fewer transistors, enabling applications to run tens to hundreds of times faster", and the GPU and TPU are given as examples. In Table 1, performance engineering of a 4096×4096 matrix multiply runs "more than 60,000 times faster" than naive Python (62,806× for version 7), and an AMD FirePro S9150 GPU runs it "360,000 times faster than the original Python code". *Relation: context.* This is the canonical statement that after Dennard, gains move from the "Bottom" (density, clock) to the "Top" (architecture and specialization), which is what the paper's specialization and precision terms measure for AI accelerators. *Checked:* PubMed record (abstract and author list), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32499413/ (via NCBI E-utilities). Full text read from a third-party mirror of the published PDF: https://www.gwern.net/doc/cs/hardware/2020-leiserson.pdf . https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aam9744 returned 403. An accepted manuscript exists at https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/159445 (not opened). DOI 10.1126/science.aam9744 verified via Crossref (368(6495), article eaam9744, 5 June 2020, seven authors). Access date 2026-10-29.

**Thompson, N. C., Greenewald, K., Lee, K., & Manso, G. F. (2020), arXiv:2007.05558 — "The computational limits of deep learning"** [`thompson2020limits`]
The authors give a back-of-the-envelope lower bound: training compute for overparameterized models "should grow at least as a fourth-order polynomial with respect to performance" (theory section). The empirical regressions find much steeper dependence. Table 2, which regresses ImageNet error on network operations, reports an "Implied Polynomial Scaling Factor" of 12.1 (95% CI 10.6–14.1) in specification (1). The hardware-burden analysis gives about 10.8 for ImageNet (text and Table 5). They note that compute in the largest systems grew "at approximately 10× per year from 2012 to 2019". Much of this "came from a much-less-economically-attractive source: running models for more time on more machines" rather than from faster hardware. The early GPU port of deep learning gave a "5–15×" speed-up, which "by 2012 had grown to more than 35×". They also state that hardware accelerators "sacrifice generality of the computing platform for the efficiency of increased specialization". *Relation: context.* This is demand-side motivation: deep learning's compute appetite outran per-chip hardware gains. The paper quantifies how much of the per-chip side came from density versus specialization and precision. *Checked:* arXiv abstract and metadata via the arXiv API (four authors; v1 10 Jul 2020, v2 27 Jul 2022), https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.05558 . Full text from https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.05558v2 . Quotes are from v2. Access date 2026-10-29.

**Khan, S. M., & Mann, A. (2020), Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET) report — "AI chips: what they are and why they matter"** [`khan2020`]
The report states: "for any given node, AI chips typically provide a 10 - 1,000x improvement in efficiency and speed relative to CPUs, with GPUs and FPGAs on the lower end and ASICs higher" (Sec. V(B)). "An AI chip a thousand times as efficient as a CPU provides an improvement equivalent to 26 years of Moore's Law-driven CPU improvements" (Summary). It attributes AI chips' advantage to parallelism, low precision (which "reduces the number of transistors needed for the same calculation"), memory optimization and domain-specific languages (Summary; Appendix B). It says that "Moore's Law is slowing" and that leading chips "have 15 times fewer transistors than they would have if Moore's Law had continued". CPU speed gains fell "to three percent per year" after 2015. CPU peak efficiency "doubled every 1.57 years until 2000", and since then "efficiency has doubled every 2.6 years" (Sec. III, "Efficiency and Speed Improvements"). *Relation: context / precedent.* This is a policy-facing statement of the paper's thesis that specialization (parallelism, precision, memory) rather than transistors explains AI-chip gains. Its CPU efficiency doubling times (1.57 → 2.6 years) are a useful contrast to the paper's 1.63-year accelerator performance per watt. *Checked:* publisher page https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/ai-chips-what-they-are-and-why-they-matter/ and full report PDF https://cset.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/AI-Chips%E2%80%94What-They-Are-and-Why-They-Matter-1.pdf (72 pp.; quotes cited by section because the PDF's printed page numbers could not be matched reliably). DOI 10.51593/20190014 verified via Crossref (type "report", April 2020, publisher CSET). Access date 2026-10-29.

## 3. Huang's law and NVIDIA's own decomposition

**Perry (2018), *IEEE Spectrum* — "Move Over, Moore's Law: Make Way for Huang's Law"** [`perry2018spectrum`]
Published 2 Apr 2018, by Tekla S. Perry. This is the article NVIDIA credits with coining the term, and it reports on Huang's GTC 2018 keynote. Its key numbers: "Nvidia's GPUs today are 25 times faster than five years ago. If they were advancing according to Moore's Law, he said, they would have increased their speed only by a factor of 10". It also gives AlexNet training going from six days on two GTX 580s to 18 minutes on DGX-2, "a factor of 500". Perry notes that Huang "seems he's still working out the exact multiple" and attributes the gains to "architecture, interconnects, memory technology, algorithms, and more". *Relation: context.* It is the origin of the "Huang's Law" name and of the 25×-in-5-years figure (~1.1-year doubling) that later sources repeat. *Checked:* https://spectrum.ieee.org/move-over-moores-law-make-way-for-huangs-law (datePublished 2018-04-02); no DOI; accessed 2026-10-29.

**Mims (2020), *The Wall Street Journal* — "Huang's Law Is the New Moore's Law, and Explains Why Nvidia Wants Arm"** [`mims2020`]
Published 2020-09-19 in the Tech section, by Christopher Mims (page metadata). Its standfirst reads: "The rule that the same dollar buys twice the computing power every 18 months is no longer true, but a new law—which we named for the CEO of Nvidia, the company now most emblematic of commercial AI—is in full effect". Only the first two paragraphs are visible before the paywall, and they contain no performance numbers. Epoch (Hobbhahn & Besiroglu 2022) cites this column for Huang's "25x improvement every 5 years" (≈1.1-year doubling). I could not see that phrase in the visible WSJ text, so I did not verify it in Mims. *Relation: context.* It is the popular source of the "Huang's Law" label that our title interrogates. *Checked:* wsj.com returned HTTP 401. Read via the Wayback snapshot https://web.archive.org/web/20200920224116/https://www.wsj.com/articles/huangs-law-is-the-new-moores-law-and-explains-why-nvidia-wants-arm-11600488001 (headline, author, date, description, lede, paywall); no DOI; accessed 2026-10-29.

**Dally, Keckler & Kirk (2021), *IEEE Micro* 41(6):42–51 — "Evolution of the Graphics Processing Unit (GPU)"** [`dally2021micro`]
Page 45 states that "GPU inference performance has improved by 317×, more than doubling each year, from 2012 (Kepler) to 2020 (Ampere)… Dubbed Huang's Law, this increase has been largely due to the addition of DL-specific data types and instructions". The source it cites for this is Huang's GTC 2018 keynote (ref. 11). The paper walks the same steps as the Hot Chips chart:
- "Kepler was 4 TFLOPS of 32-bit floating-point (FP32)"
- Pascal 10.6 TFLOPS FP32 and 21.3 TFLOPS FP16
- Volta HMMA, 125 TFLOPS FP16
- Turing IMMA, 260 TOPS INT8
- Ampere sparse MMA, "1,248 TOPS (INT8)" (p. 46)

**Dally (2021), MLSys 2021 Chips & Compilers Symposium slides — "The Future of DL Chips"** [`dally2021mlsys`] (*not cited*)
This is the earlier version of the same chart: "Single-Chip Inference Performance -317X in 8 years" (Int8 TOPS), K20X 3.94 → A100 1248. Slide 18 lists the drivers ("Number representation … Complex instructions … Process 28nm, 16nm, 7nm") without numbers. The conclusion slide states "GPU inference performance doubling every year". *Relation: precedent.* It shows that the factor list predates the numeric 2023 split and that the endpoint moved from A100 (317×) to H100 (1000×). *Checked:* https://media.mlsys.org/Conferences/MLSYS2021/Slides/DL_Chips_ML_Sys_0421.pdf (text extracted) ; https://mlsys.org/virtual/2021/symposium/1643 (programme lists Dally, "Nvidia Chips"); no DOI; accessed 2026-10-29.

**Dally (2023), *2023 IEEE Hot Chips 35 Symposium (HCS)* 1–58 — "Hardware for Deep Learning"** [`dally2023hotchips`]
Slide 11 decomposes NVIDIA's "Single-Chip Inference Performance - 1000X in 10 years" (Int8 TOPS, K20X 3.94 → H100 4000) into number representation ~16x, complex instructions ~12.5x, process ~2.5x and sparsity ~2x. It adds that "Model efficiency has also improved – overall gain > 1000x". Slide 12 gives the energy argument behind the "complex instructions" factor: per-instruction overhead is 2000% for HFMA, 500% for HDP4A, 22% for HMMA and 16% for IMMA, against "Overhead is instruction fetch, decode, and operand fetch – 30pJ" (45nm energies). Slide 23 lists future directions: log numbers, VS-Quant, activation sparsity, lower density than 2:4, and "Process • Capacitance scaling". *Relation: precedent.* This is the vendor-side multiplicative decomposition our paper tests. Dally's "process ~2.5x" is much smaller than our transistors×clock (N·f) term of 30.5× over the same K20X→H100 span. The two splits are defined differently: we account by physical quantities, he attributes by feature. *Checked:* https://hc2023.hotchips.org/program/ ; slides PDF (URL above, text extracted with pypdf) ; https://youtu.be/rsxCZAE8QNA (title only) ; Crossref https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/hcs59251.2023.10254716 — DOI verified; accessed 2026-10-29.

**Merritt (2023), *NVIDIA Blog* — "Heeding Huang's Law: Video Shows How Engineers Keep the Speedups Coming"** [`merritt2023nvblog`]
NVIDIA's write-up of the keynote, dated September 29, 2023 and written by Rick Merritt. It reports "a whopping 1,000x improvement in single GPU performance on AI inference over the past decade" and gives the 16x / 12.5x / 2x (sparsity) / 2.5x (28nm→5nm) split verbatim (see quotes above). The embedded chart "1000x-GPU-Gains-in-10-Years.jpg" is the same chart as slide 10. It credits IEEE Spectrum with first naming "Huang's Law" and says the WSJ later popularised the term. *Relation: context.* It is the accessible secondary source for the Dally factors and confirms how NVIDIA itself frames the total. *Checked:* https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/huangs-law-dally-hot-chips/ (also /blog/2023/09/29/huangs-law-dally-hot-chips/), datePublished 2023-09-29T15:00:00+00:00 in page metadata; no DOI; accessed 2026-10-29.

## 4. Trend studies built from product data

**Sun, Agostini, Dong & Kaeli (2019), arXiv:1911.11313 — "Summarizing CPU and GPU Design Trends with Product Data"** [`sun2019`]
The dataset has "4031 products (2102 CPUs and 1929 GPUs)" from Intel, AMD/ATI and NVIDIA released since 2000, sourced from "www.techpowerup.com, wikichip.org, and vendor websites". It uses base clocks only, reasoning that "devices cannot continuously work at the boost clock frequencies" (§II). Findings:
- "The frequency increase is the most important factor that drives the performance improvement of GPUs. The frequency of GPUs tripled from around 600MHz to 1.8GHz"
- Flagship TDP rose from about 150 W to about 300 W.
- Die size grew.
- "The FLOPS per watt doubles around every three to four years" (§III, §IV).

**Hobbhahn & Besiroglu (2022), Epoch AI — "Trends in GPU price-performance"** [`hobbhahn2022gpu`]
Published Jun. 27, 2022. The data are "470 models of graphics processing units released between 2006 and 2021"; "FLOP/s are those in 32-bit (full) precision". Price-performance (FLOP/s per $) doubles in:
- 2.46 years for all GPUs (95% CI 2.24–2.72)
- 2.07 years for ML GPUs (1.54–3.13)
- 2.95 years for top GPUs (2.54–3.52)

**Hobbhahn, Heim & Aydos (2023), Epoch AI — "Trends in machine learning hardware"** [`epoch2023hardware`]
Published Nov. 9, 2023. The authors are confirmed as Marius Hobbhahn, Lennart Heim and Gökçe Aydos. The dataset is "47 ML accelerators (GPUs and other AI chips) … from 2010-2023, plus 1,948 additional GPUs from 2006-2021". Table 1 doubling times:
- FP32 FLOP/s: "2x every 2.3 [2.1; 2.6] years" (N=45)
- FLOP per $ (FP32): 2.1 [1.6; 2.91] years
- FLOP/s per W: 3.0 [2.7; 3.3] years
- DRAM capacity: 4 [3; 6] years
- DRAM bandwidth: "2x every 4 [3; 5] years" (N=47); the executive summary says "every ~4.1 years"

**Rahman (2024), Epoch AI Data Insight — "The computational performance of machine learning hardware has doubled every 2.3 years"** [`rahman2024epochinsight`]
Dated Oct. 23, 2024, with data updated Nov. 24, 2025. It says: "Measured in 16-bit floating point operations, ML hardware performance has increased at a rate of 36% per year, doubling every 2.3 years. A similar trend exists in 32-bit performance. Optimized ML number formats and tensor cores provided additional improvements." It attributes the improvement to "increasing transistor counts and other semiconductor manufacturing improvements, as well as specialized design for AI workloads". A search snippet quoted different numbers for an earlier revision (2.2 years / 37%, and 2.8 years / 28%). The page I opened says 2.3 years / 36%, and that is what should be cited, with the access date. *Relation: contrast.* It is the current Epoch per-precision rate (within one format). Our 0.83-year headline doubling is faster because it lets the precision change across generations. *Checked:* https://epoch.ai/data-insights/peak-performance-hardware-on-different-precisions ; no DOI; accessed 2026-10-29.

**Epoch AI (2026), "Data on machine learning hardware" (dataset)** [`epoch2026database`]
A dataset of "key data on over 180 AI accelerators", built from hardware datasheets. A chip is included if it is "used to train a notable ML model, or are offered on a rental basis for ML workloads by major cloud compute providers". CPUs are excluded. Epoch "group[s] together BF16 and NVIDIA tensor operations at FP16 precision as 'tensor-FP16'". The page and CSV say "Updated Oct. 28, 2026", and the data are under a Creative Commons Attribution licence. Requested citation, verbatim: "Epoch AI, 'Data on machine learning hardware'. Published online at epoch.ai. Retrieved from 'https://epoch.ai/data/machine-learning-hardware' [online resource]. Accessed 29 Oct 2026." Epoch's own BibTeX uses the key `EpochMachineLearningHardware2024` but year = {2026}, month = {10}. *Relation: method.* It is our cross-check source; we should cite the version date (updated 2026-10-28, downloaded 2026-10-29). *Checked:* https://epoch.ai/data/machine-learning-hardware ; CSV at https://epoch.ai/data/ml_hardware.csv (not re-downloaded); no DOI; accessed 2026-10-29.

**Sevilla, Heim, Ho, Besiroglu, Hobbhahn & Villalobos (2022), *2022 International Joint Conference on Neural Networks (IJCNN)* 1–8 — "Compute Trends Across Three Eras of Machine Learning"** [`sevilla2022`]
From the abstract: "before 2010 training compute grew in line with Moore's law, doubling roughly every 20 months. Since the advent of Deep Learning in the early 2010s, the scaling of training compute has accelerated, doubling approximately every 6 months." It also says a Large-Scale Era began in late 2015 with "10 to 100-fold larger requirements in training compute". *Relation: context.* Demand for training compute doubles every ~6 months (0.5 years), faster even than our 0.83-year headline per-chip doubling, so chip count and spending must make up the rest. *Checked:* arXiv API (2202.05924v2; journal_ref "2022 International Joint Conference on Neural Networks (IJCNN), Padua, Italy, 2022, pp. 1-8") ; Crossref https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/IJCNN55064.2022.9891914 (authors, pages 1–8, 2022-07-18 verified); accessed 2026-10-29.

**Ho, Besiroglu, Erdil, Owen, Rahman, Guo, Atkinson, Thompson & Sevilla (2024), *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 37* 58245–58283 — "Algorithmic progress in language models"** [`ho2024`]
From the abstract: using "over 200 language model evaluations on Wikitext and Penn Treebank spanning 2012-2023", "the compute required to reach a set performance threshold has halved approximately every 8 months, with a 95% confidence interval of around 5 to 14 months, substantially faster than hardware gains per Moore's Law". It also finds that "the increase in compute made an even larger contribution to overall performance improvements". *Relation: context.* It is the software-side counterpart to Dally's "Model efficiency has also improved – overall gain > 1000x" caveat, and it marks gains our hardware decomposition deliberately excludes. *Checked:* arXiv API (2403.05812v1, 2024-03-09) ; Crossref https://api.crossref.org/works/10.52202/079017-1856 (NeurIPS 37, pp. 58245–58283, 9 authors verified); accessed 2026-10-29.

## 5. Memory wall, TPUs and number formats

**Gholami, Yao, Kim, Hooper, Mahoney & Keutzer (2024), *IEEE Micro* 44(3):33–39 — "AI and Memory Wall"** [`gholami2024`]
The abstract states: "Over the past 20 years, peak server hardware FLOPS has been scaling at 3.0x/2yrs, outpacing the growth of DRAM and interconnect bandwidth, which have only scaled at 1.6 and 1.4 times every 2 years, respectively." So 1.6×/2 yrs is the DRAM figure only; interconnect is 1.4×/2 yrs. Section I gives the cumulative version: peak compute "has increased by 60,000× over the past 20 years, as opposed to a 100× increase for DRAM or a 30× increase for interconnect bandwidth". The Fig. 2(a) caption adds that Transformer parameter counts grew "410× every two years, while the single GPU memory has only been scaled at a rate of 2× every 2 years". Section I gives training compute as 750×/2 yrs over 2018–2022. Converted to doubling times (my arithmetic, not the paper's): 3.0×/2 yr is 1.26 yr, 1.6×/2 yr is 2.95 yr, 1.4×/2 yr is 4.12 yr, and 2×/2 yr is 2.0 yr. *Relation: precedent.* This paper's flagship rates match Gholami et al. closely: 1.23 yr for headline throughput is ≈3.09×/2 yr, and 2.70 yr for bandwidth is ≈1.67×/2 yr. ## 7. Check of NVIDIA's four factors (pre-registration, estimator (c))

The pre-registration takes NVIDIA's account of the K20X → H100 gain from Dally's Hot Chips 2023 keynote. The
factors were checked against the slide deck itself (text extracted from the PDF, slide 11, "Gains from"):
"Number Representation • FP32, FP16, Int8 • (TF32, BF16) • ~16x", "Complex Instructions • DP4, HMMA, IMMA •
~12.5x", "Process • 28nm, 16nm, 7nm, 5nm • ~2.5x", "Sparsity • ~2x", and "Model efficiency has also improved –
overall gain > 1000x". The four values multiply to 1,000 and match the pre-registration, so no deviation is
needed. Four points of precision, all reflected in the paper:

- The chart ("Single-Chip Inference Performance - 1000X in 10 years") plots peak Int8-equivalent TOPS, K20X 3.94
  to H100 4000. The A100 point (1248) includes structured sparsity (Dally, Keckler & Kirk 2021, p. 46), so the
  H100 point is a sparse figure too; the dense comparator for this paper's 501× is NVIDIA's 1000× ÷ 2 = 500×.
- The slide prints no dates; 2012 and 2022 are read off the chart axis.
- The slide's last line credits part of the overall gain to model efficiency, which is outside a chip's peak rate.
- NVIDIA's blog post on the talk (Merritt) is dated 2023-09-29; the talk was on 2023-08-29.

Earlier versions give no numeric split: the MLSys 2021 slides list the same drivers without numbers ("317X in 8
years"), and the IEEE Micro paper reports 317× from 2012 to 2020 without a breakdown.

## Corrections to the brief and pre-registration wording

- Moore (1975) did not set the two-year cadence at once: he forecast that the slope "might approximate a doubling
  every two years, rather than every year, by the end of the decade". The same paper split the growth into die
  area, finer dimensions and "circuit and device cleverness", an early multiplicative decomposition.
- Koomey et al. (2011) give 1.57 years for 1946–2009 (1.52 years for PCs since 1975); "about 1.5 years" is the
  abstract's rounding.
- Gholami et al. (2024): peak FLOPS 3.0× per two years, DRAM bandwidth 1.6× and interconnect bandwidth 1.4×. Their
  series is server hardware since 1998, not accelerators only, so the agreement with this paper's flagship rates
  (headline 1.23-year doubling, bandwidth 2.70-year) is between independent estimates, not a replication.
- Epoch AI's hardware report gives the tensor-FP16 multiple over FP32 as "roughly 10x" in its summary and "~8x"
  in its body; the paper cites neither figure.
- The Epoch AI database key is `epoch2026database`, because the version used is dated October 2026.
