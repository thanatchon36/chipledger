Abstract

The peak throughput that vendors publish for their data-centre AI accelerators has grown far faster than the transistor counts behind it, a pattern that has been called Huang’s law. We ask how much of that growth is Moore’s law. From vendor documents we build a dataset of 79 accelerators from 14 vendors, announced 2012–2026, with a source, an access date and a confidence level for each of its 1,152 values, and split headline peak throughput exactly into transistor density, die area, clock, operations per transistor per cycle at 16-bit precision (specialization) and the gain from narrower number formats (precision). The design was pre-registered before any value was collected. Over the 9 flagships for which every term is published, headline dense throughput doubled every 0.83 years (95% CI 0.62–1.13) and transistor density every 2.76 years. From NVIDIA’s K20X to its H100, headline throughput rose 501×; density accounts for 33% of that gain on a log scale, specialization for 34%, clock for 16%, precision for 11% and die area for 6%. Moore’s law in the density sense is therefore about a third of Huang’s law, a share that sits just under our pre-registered threshold. Our total agrees with NVIDIA’s own account of the same decade, but the split does not: transistors times clock grew 30.5×, against the 2.5× NVIDIA credits to process, because NVIDIA counts the extra transistors as architecture. In the cross-vendor regression we cannot tell the architectural and arithmetic terms apart from density, so one pre-registered hypothesis is falsified. Memory bandwidth doubled every 2.70 years, and per-accelerator MLPerf results grew more slowly than headline peaks on all four NVIDIA benchmarks we could follow. Since H100, no vendor in the sample has published both die area and clock for its flagship, so the full decomposition ends in 2022.

1 Introduction

In 1965 Gordon Moore predicted that the number of components on an integrated circuit would keep doubling every year [1], and in 1975 he forecast that the rate would slow to a doubling every two years [2]. Fifty years later the fastest-growing number in computing is not a transistor count but the peak throughput printed on the datasheets of AI accelerators. NVIDIA’s chief scientist has described a thousand-fold gain in single-chip inference throughput over the decade from Kepler to Hopper [3], and the pattern has been called “Huang’s law” since 2018 [4], [5]. If peak throughput grows much faster than transistor counts, most of the growth must come from somewhere other than the process node, and it matters where. Process gains have been steady for half a century, whereas the alternatives (larger dies, higher clocks, more arithmetic per transistor, narrower number formats) each have a limit that a forecast of AI compute would need to know about.

This paper asks a narrow, checkable version of the question: how much of the growth in the vendor-published peak throughput of data-centre AI accelerators is Moore’s law? We answer it with an accounting identity rather than a model. For every accelerator whose vendor publishes the transistor count, the die area, the clock and the peak rates, the logarithm of the headline peak rate splits exactly into five terms: transistor density, die area, clock, specialization (operations per transistor per cycle at a 16-bit reference precision) and a precision multiplier (the gain from formats narrower than 16-bit). Moore’s law in its scaling sense is the density term. An identity has two advantages over a regression on chip features. It is exact for every product, so the shares it reports are bookkeeping facts about the published numbers, with no residual and no model to believe; and its terms correspond to the mechanisms that forecasts argue about: the process node (density), the size of the silicon (area), Dennard scaling and its end (clock), what the architecture does with each transistor (specialization) and the number formats the arithmetic runs at (precision). Its cost is that it needs five published inputs per product, and, as we show, vendors have stopped publishing two of them.

Our answer is that density supplies about a third of the growth (Figure 2). From K20X to H100, the headline rate rose 501×, or 8.97 doublings in 9.4 years, of which density supplied 2.93 and specialization 3.04. Across vendors, the flagships’ headline rate doubled every 0.83 years and their density every 2.76 years (Figure 1). The precision multiplier, often named as the main driver, moved in single steps rather than along a trend. The decade’s largest architectural gains came from matrix units at 16-bit precision. The result agrees in total with NVIDIA’s own decomposition of the same decade and disagrees with it on where the transistors belong (Section 5.3). One pre-registered hypothesis fails: with nine fully documented products, a cross-vendor regression cannot show that the architectural terms outgrow density, even though the chain within one vendor puts them ahead.

We make three contributions. The first is a dataset of 79 accelerators, each value with a source, an access date and a confidence level, released with the code under open licences. The second is a pre-registered decomposition, reported whatever the outcome; two of the seven hypotheses fail. The third is a quantitative reconciliation with the vendor’s own account, which shows that the two accountings differ in bookkeeping rather than in fact.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 places the question among the trend studies and the vendor’s own statements. Section 3 describes the dataset, how it was collected and what it does not cover. Section 4 states the identity, works it through for one product, and sets out the three estimators and the seven hypotheses. Section 5 reports the results in the order of the research questions: how fast the peaks grew, where the growth came from, how our split compares with NVIDIA’s, how efficiency and memory moved, and whether measured throughput followed the peaks. Section 6 reports the sensitivity analyses, and Sections 7 to 9 discuss, qualify and conclude.

2 Background

Moore, Dennard and Koomey. Moore’s 1965 paper projected the number of components per chip at minimum cost [1]. In 1975 he split the growth of chip complexity into larger dies, finer dimensions and a residual of “circuit and device cleverness”, the first decomposition of this kind, and forecast the two-year cadence usually quoted [2]. His own numbers are worth recalling, because the exercise below repeats them for a later decade: from 1959 to 1975 he credited a factor of about 20 to chip area and about 32 to finer dimensions, which left a residual of about 100 for cleverness, and he expected that residual to be nearly exhausted, with “another factor of four” at most still to come [2]. Dennard and colleagues showed that shrinking a MOSFET’s dimensions and voltage together keeps power density constant [6]; when voltage scaling stalled in the mid-2000s, clock rates stopped rising and the gains from a new node had to be spent on more cores or more specialised logic. Esmaeilzadeh and colleagues modelled the consequence, “dark silicon”: under a fixed power budget a growing fraction of a chip’s transistors must be switched off, more than half of them at the 8 nm node in their projection [7]. Koomey and colleagues measured the efficiency of computing, peak computations per kilowatt-hour, and found that it doubled every 1.57 years from 1946 to 2009 [8]. Nagy and colleagues hindcast 62 technologies and found that an exponential trend in time forecasts nearly as well as Wright’s law, while warning that short, noisy series fit it only loosely [9]; a fourteen-year panel of accelerators is such a series.

Specialization as the successor to scaling. Leiserson and colleagues argued that after Moore’s law, performance must come from “the Top” of the computing stack, software, algorithms and hardware architecture, and that domain-specific hardware can run applications tens to hundreds of times faster by using fewer transistors [10]. They also dated the end of the clock era: clock speed rose more than two hundred-fold from 1985 to 2005 and then plateaued with the end of Dennard scaling, and they did not expect it to rise again. Thompson and colleagues showed that the computation used by deep learning grew far faster than per-chip hardware, mostly by running models longer on more machines [11]. Jouppi and colleagues documented Google’s TPUs, the clearest case of a domain-specific accelerator built for machine learning [12], [13]. Khan and Mann explained for a policy audience why such chips outperform general-purpose processors, by parallelism, low precision, memory organisation and domain-specific languages, and put the advantage at ten to a thousand times for a given process node [14].

Matrix instructions and number formats. Two of the mechanisms in that list are the architectural terms of our identity, and the vendor literature describes both. The first TPU was built around a matrix unit of 65,536 eight-bit multiply–accumulators with a peak of 92 TOPS at 700 MHz on a 28 nm process [12]. On the GPU side, Dally, Keckler and Kirk trace the same path: Kepler at 4 TFLOP/s of FP32, Pascal adding a 16-bit vector rate, Volta’s HMMA matrix instruction at 125 TFLOP/s of FP16, Turing’s IMMA at INT8 and Ampere’s sparse matrix instructions [15]. Dally’s Hot Chips keynote gives the energy reason a matrix instruction raises the work done per transistor: instruction fetch, decode and operand fetch cost about twenty times the arithmetic of a scalar 16-bit fused multiply–add, but only about a fifth of the arithmetic of an HMMA and about a sixth of an IMMA, so a matrix instruction amortises the overhead over many multiply–accumulates [3]. Narrower formats are the other mechanism. Micikevicius and colleagues proposed the two FP8 encodings, E4M3 and E5M2, with the stated aim of “smaller and more power efficient math pipelines” and lower memory bandwidth pressure, and reported FP8 training that matched 16-bit results on models of up to 175 billion parameters [16]. Each halving of the operand width lets the same arithmetic area do about twice the operations per cycle, which is why the precision term of the identity moves in steps of one doubling.

Huang’s law and the vendor’s decomposition. IEEE Spectrum named “Huang’s law” in 2018, reporting Jensen Huang’s claim that NVIDIA’s GPUs were 25 times faster than five years earlier, where Moore’s law would have given a factor of 10 [4]; the Wall Street Journal put the term in a headline in 2020 [5]. Dally, Keckler and Kirk reported a 317× gain in GPU inference performance from Kepler in 2012 to Ampere in 2020, “largely due to the addition of DL-specific data types and instructions”, and expected it to slow toward the 35% a year that technology alone provides [15]. Dally’s Hot Chips 2023 keynote extended the chart to H100 and split its thousand-fold gain into number representation, complex instructions, process and sparsity [3], [17]. That decomposition is the vendor account we test in Section 5.3.

Trend studies. Sun and colleagues fitted design trends for more than four thousand CPUs and GPUs from product data and found clock frequency to be the main driver of GPU FLOPS growth, measured at FP32 and base clock, with GPU frequencies tripling from about 600 MHz to 1.8 GHz over their window and FLOPS per watt doubling every three to four years [18]. Epoch AI estimated that the FP32 price-performance of GPUs doubles about every 2.5 years [19], and that the FP32 peak of machine learning hardware doubles every 2.3 years and its memory bandwidth every 4 years, with tensor formats giving a further, roughly constant multiple at each precision [20], [21]. Epoch also maintains the hardware database we use as a cross-check [22]. Sevilla and colleagues measured the growth of training compute [23], and Ho and colleagues the algorithmic progress that adds to it [24]; both are demand-side counterparts of the supply side studied here. None of these studies splits a chip’s peak rate into an exact sum of physical and architectural terms with a source for every input, and none reconciles a vendor’s own account.

Memory wall and realized performance. Gholami and colleagues showed that the peak compute of server hardware has outgrown memory and interconnect bandwidth for two decades, at 3.0× every two years against 1.6× for DRAM bandwidth and 1.4× for interconnect bandwidth [25]. Jouppi and colleagues reported that four of the six production workloads on the first TPU were memory-bound, so that a chip with a peak of 92 TOPS delivered 21.4 on average [12], and split one TPU generation’s peak gain into more matrix units and a higher clock while memory bandwidth rose much less [13]. MLPerf provides measured training and inference throughput on fixed tasks [26], [27], which we use to check whether realized performance follows the headline peaks. Large training runs report the same gap in another unit: Chowdhery and colleagues define model FLOPs utilization as observed throughput over the throughput the vendor peak would allow, and tabulate values between about a fifth and a half for four large language models [28].

3 Data

Unit and population. The unit is a product: a distinct silicon configuration sold or deployed as a data-centre machine learning accelerator, announced between 2012 and 2026. Variants of the same silicon that differ in memory, power or form factor are separate rows linked to a primary variant, the highest-TDP module form; only primary variants enter the analyses. Throughput, bandwidth and transistors are counted per package. A board that carries two packages is recorded as one package (NVIDIA’s K80 as one of its two GK210 GPUs, Tenstorrent’s Wormhole n300 as its single-ASIC sibling n150), so that a product is compared with others on the same unit. The dataset has 79 products from 14 vendors, 66 of them primary. Within each vendor and architecture generation, the flagship is the primary part with the highest headline dense rate among parts positioned for training; there are 44. One card whose rates are given for an unstated number of chips is excluded from every analysis.

Fields. For each product we record the announce and ship dates, process node and foundry, transistor count, compute and total die area, number of compute dies, boost clock, TDP, memory capacity and bandwidth, and every published peak rate: vector FP64, FP32, FP16, BF16 and INT8, and matrix-unit rates from FP64 down to FP4 and INT4, dense and, separately, with structured sparsity. Vector and matrix-unit rates at the same precision are separate columns, because the specialization term is meant to capture the matrix units. Prices are recorded where a vendor publishes them; none does for the products in the sample, so no per-dollar analysis is possible.

Collection. The pre-registration lists a seed of products by name and an inclusion rule. For each product the vendor’s datasheet, whitepaper, product page or paper was opened and every value transcribed by hand with its URL, the access date and a confidence level. Fifteen products beyond the seed were added because they met the inclusion rule, and three were left out because no vendor document gives a peak rate; both lists are in the deviations log. Values that a vendor states only indirectly are recorded as derived and carry the vendor’s sentence in their note: the main case is dense matrix rates given as half of the sparse rate where the vendor’s own page says so. Where two documents disagree, the vendor’s datasheet wins and the alternatives are listed in data/conflicts.md. A validator requires every value in the table to equal a row of the source table, so a source cannot be present without matching the value it is supposed to support.

Sources. Every value carries a URL, an access date and a confidence level in data/sources.csv. The order of preference is the vendor’s datasheet, whitepaper or product page, then a vendor-authored paper, then Epoch AI’s hardware database [22] at medium confidence where the vendor is silent. Of the 1,152 source rows, 1,123 are high confidence, 24 medium and 5 low. The medium rows are mostly boost clocks that NVIDIA states in no document we could find and that Epoch AI carries from a third-party database; a sensitivity analysis drops every value below high confidence. Where sources disagree, the vendor’s document wins and the alternatives are listed in data/conflicts.md.

Decisions taken during collection. Four decisions were taken at review gates after the pre-registration was frozen, each recorded in DEVIATIONS.md with its date, reason and effect. (i) Dense rates may be derived as half of the published sparse rate where the vendor’s own page states that rule; without it, H100, B200 and B300 would enter the sample at their FP32 rates, because NVIDIA lists their tensor rates with sparsity only. (ii) Epoch AI’s database may supply a value at medium confidence where no vendor document gives it, because the pre-registered fallback for clocks refused every automated request. (iii) Throughput is counted per package, which makes K40 rather than the two-GPU K80 the Kepler flagship, and the highest-TDP module is the primary variant, which makes MI325X rather than MI300X the primary row of that silicon; a sensitivity analysis with MI300X as the primary gives the same chain shares. (iv) A column for vector INT8 rates was added to the frozen schema, because the headline is defined over every published format and AMD’s MI60 publishes a vector INT8 rate twice its FP16 rate. None of these changes a definition or a decision rule.

Coverage. The decomposition needs five fields (transistor count, compute die area, clock, TDP and a reference rate), and publication of these has declined. NVIDIA has not published die area or boost clock for Blackwell or Rubin, AMD has not published die area since MI100, and Google publishes neither transistor count nor die area for its TPUs. Only 9 flagships have all five fields; 15 have every field except die area and 11 every field except the clock. We use the last two groups with reduced identities (Section 4). data/coverage.md lists, for every product, the fields it lacks. Table 1 lists the 9 products of the decomposition sample with their five inputs. They run from K40’s 5.0 TFLOP/s of vector FP32 to H100’s 1,979 TFLOP/s of FP8 matrix arithmetic, and include one wafer-scale part, Cerebras’ WSE-2, whose 46,225 mm² and 2,600 billion transistors are two orders of magnitude above the rest; it is kept in every fit and dropped in a sensitivity analysis.

Measured throughput. For the comparison of peaks with measured performance we collected MLPerf results for NVIDIA’s flagships from the published result tables, in data/mlperf.csv with the URL of each result. For training, the metric is time to train to a fixed quality target [26], and we convert it to a per-accelerator throughput as the inverse of the product of training time and the number of accelerators in the submitted system. For inference, we take the offline scenario, in which data are available in advance and an accelerator can reach and hold its peak [27], and divide the system’s samples per second by its accelerator count. For each product and benchmark the pre-registered rule takes the first round in which the product appears, the result closest to launch software; the best result over all rounds is reported alongside. Rows from a benchmark whose definition changed between rounds are kept in the file with the reason and left out of the comparison.

Process nodes. To ask whether accelerators reach new nodes later than they used to, we also compiled, for each foundry node in the dataset, the year in which the foundry’s own statements put it into volume production, and mapped each vendor’s custom node name onto the foundry node it is derived from. The lag is the announce year minus the middle of the volume-production year.

4 Method

Definitions. The headline dense rate PP is the largest dense peak rate over every supported format, INT8 and INT4 included, counting two operations per multiply–accumulate. The reference rate RR is the dense matrix-unit FP16 or BF16 rate; if the product has no matrix unit, its vector FP16 or BF16 rate; if it has no 16-bit format, its FP32 rate. NN is the transistor count of the package, AA the total area of its compute dies, ff the boost clock (the clock at which vendors state their peak rates, unlike the base clock used by Sun and colleagues [18]) and WW the TDP.

Identity A. For every product with all five fields, by construction, log⁡2P=log⁡2NA+log⁡2A+log⁡2f+log⁡2RNf+log⁡2PR,\log_2 P = \log_2 \frac{N}{A} + \log_2 A + \log_2 f + \log_2 \frac{R}{N f} + \log_2 \frac{P}{R}, that is, density + area + clock + specialization + precision. Specialization is reference operations per transistor per cycle: it rises when more of the silicon does 16-bit arithmetic every cycle, and falls when transistors go to caches, I/O or features that do not raise RR. Sparsity is outside PP. The identity holds in any consistent units; we use millions of transistors per mm² for density, mm² for area, MHz for the clock and TFLOP/s for the rates, so that the five factors multiply to PP in TFLOP/s without a constant.

A worked example. H100 SXM5 publishes 80 billion transistors on 814 mm² of compute die, a boost clock of 1,980 MHz, a dense FP16 matrix rate of 989.5 TFLOP/s and a dense FP8 matrix rate of 1,979 TFLOP/s, its headline. Its density is therefore 98.3 million transistors per mm², its specialization 6.25 reference operations per million transistors per cycle, and its precision multiplier 2, because FP8 is one halving below the 16-bit reference. In base-two logarithms the identity reads 6.62 (density) + 9.67 (area) + 10.95 (clock) −17.29 (specialization) + 1.00 (precision) = 10.95, which is log⁡2\log_2 of the headline rate. The specialization term is a small number because a transistor does far less than one operation per cycle; what matters is its change between products.

Reading the terms. Density is Moore’s law in its scaling sense, and the product of density and area, the transistor count, is Moore’s law in its original sense [1]. The clock is the term Dennard scaling drove and its end froze [6], [10]. Specialization and precision are Leiserson’s “Top”: specialization is what the microarchitecture does with each transistor in each cycle, and rises with matrix units, wider datapaths and a larger fraction of the die devoted to arithmetic; precision is the number of operations a datapath does at the headline format relative to the 16-bit reference, and rises by one doubling for each halving of the operand width. Because the terms multiply, shares are taken on the logarithmic scale: the share of a term over an interval is its change in log⁡2\log_2 divided by the change in log⁡2P\log_2 P. Shares sum to one, and a share can be negative, as precision’s is on the step from A100 to H100, whose headline format widened from INT4 to FP8.

Reduced identities. Two reduced identities cover products with missing fields. Identity A′ merges density and area into the transistor count, log⁡2P=log⁡2N+log⁡2f+log⁡2(R/Nf)+log⁡2(P/R)\log_2 P = \log_2 N + \log_2 f + \log_2 (R/Nf) + \log_2 (P/R), for products without a published die area; its transistor term is Moore’s law in the original sense. The clock-free identity merges the clock into specialization, which becomes reference operations per transistor per second, R/NR/N, for products without a published clock. Identity B splits PP into P/WP/W and WW, throughput per watt and the power budget, and identity C into P/AP/A and AA, throughput per mm² and the silicon area; both are reported for the NVIDIA chain.

Estimator (a): the chain. The chain links the first flagship of each NVIDIA architecture from K20X (Kepler) onward: K20X, M40, P100, V100, A100 and H100, named in the pre-registration. For each step it reports the change in each term’s log⁡2\log_2, in doublings, and for the whole chain each term’s cumulative change and share. The chain cannot be extended past H100 under identity A because NVIDIA publishes neither die area nor boost clock for Blackwell or Rubin; identity B, which needs only TDP, is carried to B300. A second chain follows AMD’s Instinct flagships from MI60 under identity A′, because AMD has not published die area since MI100.

Estimator (b): the regression. For every flagship with all five fields we regress log⁡2\log_2 of each term and of PP on the decimal announce year by least squares. Because the identity is additive in logarithms and least squares is linear, the five term slopes sum exactly to the slope of PP, so the regression is a decomposition of the cross-vendor trend in the same way the chain is a decomposition of one vendor’s steps. The doubling time of a series is the inverse of its slope. Uncertainty comes from a bootstrap over chips: products are resampled with replacement, 2,000 times from a fixed seed, and the same resampled set is used for every series in a draw, so that a contrast between slopes, such as specialization + precision − density for H3 or headline − bandwidth for H5, is computed within each draw and its interval is the percentile interval of those differences. Heteroscedasticity-robust (HC3) standard errors and the Theil–Sen slope are reported as checks. The same estimator is run under identity A′ and the clock-free identity on the larger samples those identities allow.

Estimator (c): the reconciliation. NVIDIA’s account of the same decade [3] multiplies four factors, number representation, complex instructions, process and sparsity. We compute identity A from K20X to H100 at the endpoint rates NVIDIA’s chart uses, K20X’s FP32 rate and H100’s FP8 rate, drop sparsity from both sides, and compare the physical part of the identity, density × area × clock, which is NfN f, with NVIDIA’s “process” factor, and its architectural part, specialization × precision, with NVIDIA’s number representation × complex instructions. Because NVIDIA’s number representation factor starts from FP32, the same comparison is repeated with FP32 as every chip’s reference rate.

Hypotheses. Seven hypotheses were pre-registered with decision rules; Table 5 repeats the rules next to the outcomes. In short:

  • H1, speed: the flagship headline rate doubles in at most 1.5 years, with the upper bound of its interval below 2 years.
  • H2, Moore’s law is the minority: density doubles in at least 2.5 years and supplies less than a third of the NVIDIA chain’s gain.
  • H3, architecture outweighs density: specialization and precision together exceed density, in the chain’s shares and in the regression, where the contrast’s interval must exclude zero.
  • H4, bigger silicon and more watts after 2020: on the NVIDIA chain, die area and TDP grow more per step after 2020 than before.
  • H5, the memory wall: the headline slope exceeds the bandwidth slope and the interval of the difference excludes zero.
  • H6, exploratory: per-accelerator MLPerf throughput grows more slowly than the headline rate on every benchmark with three generations.
  • H7, the reconciliation: from K20X to H100, transistors × clock grew at least 10×, four times NVIDIA’s process factor.

Pre-registration. The question, the definitions, the sample rule, the estimators, the seven hypotheses with their decision rules and the sensitivity analyses were frozen at the git tag prereg-v1 before any value was collected. The thresholds were set after the author had worked the identity through from remembered headline figures, so the pre-registration is not blind. Every later change, including the four decisions taken at review gates during data collection, is logged with its date and reason in DEVIATIONS.md; the main ones are described where they matter below.

Reproducibility. Every number in this paper, in the text as well as in the tables, is filled from one results file by the build, which fails if a number is typed by hand; make all regenerates the results file, the figures and the paper from the committed dataset without network access. The released dataset carries the per-value provenance described above, so a reader can check any input against the vendor’s document.

5 Results

5.1 How fast

Figure 1 shows the ladder. Over all 43 flagships with a headline rate, the headline doubled every 1.23 years (95% CI 1.08–1.49), the 16-bit reference rate every 1.61 years and the vector FP32 rate every 2.68 years, slower than Epoch AI’s 2.3 years for the FP32 peak of machine learning hardware in general [20]. The three series are ordered as the identity predicts: the gap between the FP32 and the 16-bit trends is what matrix units and 16-bit datapaths added, and the gap between the 16-bit and the headline trends is what narrower formats added on top. The headline rate of a 2026 flagship is the same quantity as the FP32 rate of a 2013 flagship, two operations per multiply–accumulate, but at a narrower precision and, for some products, over several dies.

The silicon behind the rates grew more slowly, and in a different way. Transistors per package doubled every 1.87 years over 26 flagships (Figure 1(b)), close to Moore’s 1975 cadence, but transistor density, over the 12 flagships that publish both count and area, doubled only every 3.06 years (CI 2.49–3.80). The difference is area. The compute die area of single-die flagships has been nearly flat since V100 at a little over 815 mm², close to the lithographic reticle, so the transistor count of a package now grows by putting more dies in it; the two wafer-scale parts sit two orders of magnitude above that line (Figure 1(d)), which is why the area trend has the widest interval in the figure. On the 9 flagships with all five fields, the headline doubled every 0.83 years (CI 0.62–1.13), which meets H1. The headline rate has therefore doubled two to three times as fast as density, and the rest of this section asks where the difference comes from.

5.2 Where it comes from

The NVIDIA chain. Figure 2 and Table 2 give the chain decomposition. From K20X to H100, density contributed 0.327 of the change in log⁡2P\log_2 P, die area 0.063, clock 0.160, specialization 0.339 and precision 0.111. The steps differ in kind, and the table is worth reading row by row. The Kepler-to-Maxwell step, 0.78 doublings over three years on the same 28 nm node, came almost entirely from clock (0.61 doublings). Maxwell to Pascal, 1.64 doublings in less than half a year, is the chain’s one large density step (0.91 doublings, the move from 28 nm to 16 nm FinFET), with a further clock gain and the first 16-bit vector rate. Pascal to Volta added tensor cores and raised specialization by 2.05 doublings in one step, the largest single-term change in the chain, while the die grew from 610 to 815 mm². Volta to Ampere took the headline to INT4, a precision gain of 2.00 doublings, on top of 1.34 doublings of density from the move to 7 nm; specialization barely moved on that step, because Ampere’s 16-bit matrix rate rose (from 125 to 312 TFLOP/s) by about as much as its transistor count (from 21.1 to 54.2 billion). Hopper’s headline format, FP8, is one step wider than Ampere’s INT4, for which no H100 rate appears in the documents we collected, so the precision term fell by −1.00 doublings in the last step and the headline rose by only 0.67 doublings, although the 16-bit reference rate itself rose from 312 to 990 TFLOP/s on density, clock and specialization together. Die area barely moved over the whole chain because K20X was already a large die.

Transistors against density. Over the chain the transistor count grew by 3.49 doublings, the sum of density’s 2.93 and area’s 0.56. Under identity A′, which does not separate the two, the transistor term takes 39% of the NVIDIA chain, and Moore’s law in its original sense is a larger share of Huang’s law than Moore’s law in the scaling sense. AMD’s Instinct chain from MI60 to MI355X, with identity A′ because AMD does not publish die area, gives transistors 0.513, clock 0.056, specialization 0.294 and precision 0.137 of its 7.42 doublings in 6.6 years (Figure 2(b)). H2: Moore’s law proper is the minority. Density’s share of the NVIDIA chain, 0.327, is below one third, and its doubling time across flagships, 2.76 years, is above 2.5 years, so H2 is supported. The margin is thin: a change of 4.1% in the transistor count or die area of either endpoint would move the share to one third. Vendor transistor counts are rounded figures, so the honest statement is that density’s share is about a third, not that it is below one.

H3: architecture and arithmetic outweigh density. In the chain, specialization and precision together account for 0.450 of the gain against density’s 0.327, so part (a) holds. The regression (Table 3, Figure 3) tells a less clear story. Across the nine flagships, density rose at 0.363 doublings per year with the narrowest interval of any term, specialization at 0.332, precision at 0.121 with an interval that touches zero, and the clock at 0.071; area’s slope, 0.321, is pulled up by the wafer-scale part and has the widest interval. The specialization and precision slopes sum to more than the density slope by 0.090 doublings per year, but the 95% interval runs from −0.103 to 0.307, and the Theil–Sen version of the contrast is −0.026. Part (b) fails and H3 is falsified as pre-registered. With nine products, the regression cannot separate the architectural terms from density, even though the chain puts them ahead; the two estimators do not contradict each other, because the chain measures one vendor’s path and the regression a cross-vendor trend whose nine points include two parts, GC200 and WSE-2, with very different mixes of the terms.

5.3 The vendor’s account

Dally’s chart of “single-chip inference performance” plots peak INT8-equivalent TOPS from K20X to H100, and his slide attributes the gain to number representation (16.0×), complex instructions (12.5×), process (2.5×) and sparsity (2.0×), which multiply to 1,000×, or 500× without sparsity [3]. The H100 point on the chart, like the A100 point [15], is a sparse rate, and the slide adds that model efficiency raised the overall gain further; we compare dense rates and hardware only. Identity A, from K20X’s FP32 rate of 3.95 TFLOP/s to H100’s dense FP8 rate of 1,979 TFLOP/s, gives 501×: the totals agree (Figure 4, Table 4). The split does not. Transistors grew 11.3× and the clock 2.70×, so the physical factor NfNf grew 30.5×, 12.2 times NVIDIA’s process factor. This meets H7. Correspondingly, specialization × precision is 16.4×, against NVIDIA’s 200×. Because the two totals agree, the factor that moves between the physical and the architectural columns is the same on both sides, about 12×: what NVIDIA’s account adds to architecture, it takes from process.

The two accountings differ in bookkeeping. NVIDIA’s “process” is the gain per transistor from a newer node; the extra transistors that the node and a larger die make available are credited to the architecture that uses them. Identity A counts every transistor as a physical input and credits architecture only with what it does per transistor per cycle. Neither is wrong, but they answer different questions: NVIDIA’s says what the design team did with the transistors it was given; ours says how much of the gain would have arrived without any change in what a transistor does. The correspondence between the architectural terms is closer than the totals suggest. NVIDIA’s “complex instructions” are the matrix instructions whose per-operation overhead Dally’s own energy figures describe (Section 2); those instructions are what raises our specialization term, operations per transistor per cycle at the reference precision. NVIDIA’s “number representation” runs from FP32 to FP8 and so contains the move to 16-bit formats, which in our identity is part of the reference rate and hence of specialization, not of precision. Taking FP32 as the reference for every chip, which is closer to NVIDIA’s “number representation”, moves most of the architectural gain from specialization to precision (29.5× precision and 0.56× specialization, below one because an FP32 operation on H100’s matrix units costs more transistor-cycles than an FP32 operation on K20X’s vector units) but leaves the physical factor where it was. The choice of reference rate therefore moves gain between the two architectural terms and never between architecture and physics.

5.4 Efficiency and memory

Figure 5 shows three efficiency ratios. Headline throughput per watt of TDP doubled every 1.63 years over 29 flagships, the 16-bit reference rate per watt every 2.22 years, throughput per mm² of compute die every 1.27 years and per billion transistors every 2.02 years. Koomey’s efficiency trend for general computing was a doubling every 1.57 years [8]; peak AI throughput per watt has kept a similar pace, mostly through formats and matrix units rather than voltage. The gap between the headline and the reference rate per watt, 1.63 against 2.22 years, is the precision term again: at a fixed format, efficiency has improved at about the pace Epoch AI reports for FP32 FLOP/s per watt, a doubling every 3.0 years [20], and the faster headline figure comes from doing the arithmetic at narrower widths. Throughput per mm² doubles almost as fast as throughput itself, because the area of a single die has stopped growing; throughput per transistor, which is specialization × precision × clock, doubles every 2.02 years and is the cleanest cross-vendor measure of how much more each transistor does.

Memory has not kept up (Figure 6). Over 38 flagships, bandwidth doubled every 2.70 years and capacity every 2.61 years, while the headline rate doubled every 1.23 years, so the operations available per byte fetched doubled every 2.20 years (fitted directly to the ratio on the flagships that report bandwidth, so it is not exactly the difference of the two slopes). In Figure 6(a) the flagships climb from the line of about twenty operations per byte, where K40 sits, to about a thousand for the newest parts, near the ridge point of 1,350 operations per byte that Jouppi and colleagues reported for the first TPU’s roofline [12]; a workload whose arithmetic intensity is below a chip’s ratio is bandwidth-bound on it, and the figure shows the whole industry moving that ratio up. Expressed per two years, the unit Gholami and colleagues use, the headline rate grew 3.09× and bandwidth 1.67×, against their 3.0× and 1.6× for server hardware since 1998 [25]; their series is broader and longer than ours, so the two are independent estimates that agree rather than a replication. Flagship bandwidth grew faster than the four-year doubling Epoch AI found for machine learning hardware in general [20], but not as fast as compute. In the pre-registered test on the decomposition sample (n = 7), the headline slope exceeds the bandwidth slope by 0.709 doublings per year (CI 0.395–1.072), so H5 is supported; on all 38 flagships with both, the gap is 0.454 (0.349–0.530).

5.5 Peak against measured

H6 compares the headline trend with MLPerf results, per accelerator, from the first round in which each product appears. Four benchmarks have results for three NVIDIA flagship generations; no other vendor has three. On all four, the measured per-accelerator throughput grew more slowly than the headline rate of the same products (Figure 7): ResNet-50 training 0.45 against 0.85 doublings per year, BERT training 0.65 against 0.79, Llama 2 70B LoRA fine-tuning 0.58 against 1.01, and Llama 2 70B offline inference 0.83 against 1.01. The gap is widest on ResNet-50 training, the oldest benchmark, whose V100-to-H100 span includes the precision steps that raised the headline without raising the rate at which a 16-bit training job runs, and narrowest on offline inference, the scenario that Reddi and colleagues describe as the one in which an accelerator can hold its peak [27]. Taking each product’s best result over all rounds instead of its first gives the same verdict on every benchmark (0.56 doublings per year on BERT, for example). H6 was pre-registered as exploratory. With three products per benchmark, results from systems of different sizes and software of different ages, and the BERT results of the first round excluded because that round’s accuracy target was lower, it is a consistency check and not an estimate of the gap. Software moves these results too: between the first two training rounds, the best 16-chip results improved 1.3× on unchanged hardware [26], so a first-round result understates what the same chip delivers later; Google measured larger compiler-only gains over 20 months on both GPUs and TPUs [29]. Since those gains accrue to the measured series and not to the headline, the direction of the result is conservative: the measured series grows more slowly than the peaks even with the software gains on its side.

7 Discussion

Against other estimates. The headline doubling time of about a year sits where the earlier literature put it: Huang’s 25× in five years [4] and Dally’s 317× in eight [15] are both close to a doubling a year, and our 1.23 years across vendors or 0.83 years on the decomposition sample confirm them on sourced data. The FP32 series is the one comparable with the general trend studies, and it is slower: 2.68 years against Epoch AI’s 2.3 years [20], which is expected for flagships that have moved their arithmetic to narrower formats and no longer grow their FP32 datapaths. Sun and colleagues found frequency to be the main driver of GPU FLOPS since 2000 [18]; over our decade the clock still multiplied NVIDIA’s headline by 2.7×, a sixth of the chain’s gain, but its cross-vendor trend, 0.07 doublings per year, says that the era they describe was ending as our window opened. On memory, our per-two-year factors, 3.09× for compute and 1.67× for bandwidth, agree with Gholami and colleagues’ 3.0× and 1.6× [25] to within their rounding; our capacity figure, 1.70× per two years, is below the 2× they give for single-GPU memory. Khan and Mann’s ten-to-a-thousand-fold advantage of AI chips over CPUs at a given node [14] is a comparison across chip types; the within-line counterpart here is the 16.4× that specialization and precision together added to NVIDIA’s flagship over a decade at the same vendor.

Moore’s own decomposition, fifty years on. Moore’s 1975 split of the previous sixteen years gave the residual, “circuit and device cleverness”, the largest share, about 100× against about 32× for finer dimensions and about 20× for area, and he expected that residual to run out [2]. Our split of 2012–2022 for accelerators has the same shape: the terms that are not density or area, clock, specialization and precision, multiply to more than density and area do, and one of them, precision, has a visible floor. The difference is that Moore’s residual was a correction to a density forecast, whereas for accelerators the residual is the larger part of the trend that is being forecast.

What is left. The terms of identity A have different futures. Density is the slow, steady term. The clock has nearly stopped: its trend across flagships is 0.07 doublings per year. Precision moves in single steps, and they run out: 8-bit floating-point formats for deep learning [16] reached the headline with Hopper, and four-bit formats with Blackwell. Each halving of the operand width at most doubles the peak rate, and the headline formats of current flagships are four bits wide, so at most one or two such steps remain before arithmetic reaches two bits or one. The area of a single die is bounded by the lithographic reticle, which H100 already approaches; growth since then has come from packages with more than one die, which raises the transistor count and, by identity B, power. Specialization has been the largest term after density, and it is the one with no obvious bound, though its decade was dominated by a single invention, the matrix unit at 16-bit precision, and the chain shows it arriving in one step rather than along a trend.

What this means for forecasts. A forecast that extrapolates the headline doubling time of about a year assumes that the non-density terms keep contributing as they did. Two of them, precision and clock, cannot contribute at that rate for long, and a third, single-die area, is near its limit. If density keeps doubling every two to three years and specialization keeps its pace, the headline rate would double more slowly than in 2012–2022, unless packaging and power budgets keep growing. This is an argument about the composition of the trend, not a forecast; our data do not identify a break. They also show that the headline rate overstates the growth of measured throughput (Section 5.5), and that the memory system falls further behind every year (Section 5.4). A forecast of delivered AI compute should therefore extrapolate the measured series, or the 16-bit reference rate, rather than the headline, and should carry the memory trend alongside.

Disclosure. The most practical finding may be the coverage problem. In 2012–2020 NVIDIA published transistor count, die area and clock for every flagship. For Blackwell and Rubin it publishes transistor counts but neither die area nor boost clock, and other vendors publish less: AMD has given no die area since MI100 and Google no transistor count or die area for any TPU. An exact accounting of where AI hardware gains come from will soon be impossible from public documents unless vendors publish these five fields again. The reduced identities soften the problem but do not solve it: identity A′ cannot separate density from area, and the clock-free identity cannot separate the clock from architecture.

8 Limitations

Peaks are not performance. The rates are vendor-published peaks, not measured throughput; Section 5.5 shows that the two diverge. Large training runs report a model FLOPs utilization of about 21–46% of the same peaks [28], so a doubling of peak need not double delivered work. Our decomposition explains the number on the datasheet, which is the number Huang’s law is about, and not the work a chip does.

Definitions vary across vendors. Vendors define peak rates in slightly different ways (boost or sustained clock, with or without the sparsity factor), and we took them as published, recording derived values as such. The boost clock is the clock NVIDIA states its peaks at; where a vendor publishes one clock we used it, and the 24 medium-confidence values, mostly clocks from Epoch AI’s database, are dropped in one sensitivity analysis without changing the conclusions. The choice of the 16-bit reference rate moves gain between specialization and precision (Table 4) and does not move the physical factor; a reader who prefers FP32 as the baseline should read the two architectural terms together.

The sample is small and one vendor dominates it. The decomposition sample is 9 flagships, 6 of them NVIDIA’s, and it ends in 2022; the chain is one vendor’s product line, frozen by name in the pre-registration. The intervals reflect sampling over chips, not the measurement error in vendor figures, which are rounded. TPU and most post-2022 products enter only the throughput, efficiency and memory trends. The regression’s failure to separate the architectural terms from density (H3b) is a failure to detect, not evidence of absence.

Dates and prices. Announce dates are the primary dates because ship dates are published for few products; the two give similar doubling times where both exist. No vendor publishes prices, so we could not compare performance per dollar with Epoch AI’s estimates [19], and the identity says nothing about the cost of a transistor, a package or a watt.

The pre-registration was not blind. The author knew the headline figures of NVIDIA’s flagships when setting the thresholds. Two decision rules were close (H2b and the ship-date variant), and H2b’s margin is within the rounding of vendor transistor counts. The MLPerf comparison uses three products per benchmark and one vendor, with systems of different sizes and software of different ages; it checks a direction and does not measure a gap.

9 Conclusion

From 2012 to 2022, the vendor-published peak throughput of AI accelerators doubled about every year, and transistor density, Moore’s law in its scaling sense, supplied about a third of that growth. The rest came mostly from specialization at 16-bit precision, then from clock and narrower formats, with die area contributing little. NVIDIA’s own account of the same decade agrees in total; it differs in counting the extra transistors as architecture. Huang’s law is therefore not Moore’s law, but neither is it independent of it: without density, the decade’s gain would have been about a third smaller on a log scale, and without the transistors that density and area together supplied, about two fifths smaller. Whether the next decade looks the same depends on terms that the vendors have stopped publishing.
